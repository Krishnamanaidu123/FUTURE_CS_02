Exhibit A

Identifying suspicious emails

Phishing emails are built to trigger a fast, unthinking click. Click the numbered markers below to see what a trained eye catches in the first ten seconds.

Evidence 01 — Inbox
From IT Support Desk <it-support@secure-office365-verify.com>1
To you@yourcompany.com
Subject Action Required: Your mailbox will be suspended in 24 hours2
Look-alike domain. "secure-office365-verify.com" is not Microsoft — it mimics a trusted name to slip past a quick glance.
Manufactured urgency. Real IT deadlines rarely hinge on a single day, and legitimate suspensions don't get announced by email alone.
Dear User, We detected unusual sign-in activity on your account. To avoid permanent loss of access3, please verify your identity immediately by clicking the link below. http://office365-login.verify-secure.net/auth4 Failure to verify within 24 hours will result in account suspension. Thanks, The Microsoft Team5
Fear-based framing. Threats of loss (access, money, your job) are designed to override careful thinking — a hallmark of pressure tactics, not IT policy.
Mismatched link. Hovering (never clicking) shows a domain unrelated to Microsoft, with no padlock guarantee and an unfamiliar path.
Generic, impersonal sign-off. Genuine IT teams usually reference your name, ticket number, or company — not a vague "the team."

Sender mismatch

The display name says one thing, the actual address says another. Always check the full email address, not just the name shown.

Display: "PayPal Security" → Address: security@paypa1-support.ru

Urgency & threats

Countdown timers, account suspensions, and "final notice" language exist to short-circuit your judgment before you check the facts.

Generic greetings

"Dear Customer" or "Dear User" instead of your name often signals a mass-sent scam rather than an institution that actually holds your data.

Odd attachments

Unexpected invoices, "resumes," or shipping labels — especially .zip, .exe, .js, or macro-enabled Office files — are common malware delivery methods.

Exhibit B

Spotting fake login pages & fraudulent sites

A cloned login page can be visually perfect. The tell is almost never the design — it's the address bar and the way the page was reached.

Evidence 02 — Address bar
https://accounts-google-secure.com1/signin?continue=verify-account-update2
The real domain isn't google.com — it's a domain that merely contains the word "google" as a subdomain-like prefix. Only what comes immediately before the first single slash after "https://" counts.
Long, unfamiliar parameter strings trying to look official are often decoration meant to distract from the domain itself.

Read the domain right-to-left

In login.microsoft.verify-id.net, the real domain is verify-id.net. Everything before it is just a decorative subdomain.

No padlock, or a mismatched one

HTTPS is now trivial for scammers to obtain too, so a padlock alone proves nothing — but its total absence on a "bank" page is still a red flag.

Arrived via link, not typed

If you reached a login page by clicking an email or ad link rather than typing the address or using a bookmark, treat the page as unverified.

Pre-filled or auto-submit forms

Some fake pages auto-fill your email to look personalized, or submit on a single field to capture partial credentials before you notice anything wrong.

Exhibit C

Social engineering techniques

Phishing is a psychology exercise wearing a technical costume. These are the pressure points criminals reliably lean on.

  • 01
    Authority impersonation

    Posing as a CEO, bank, tax authority, or IT admin so the request feels too risky to question.

  • 02
    Urgency & scarcity

    "Offer expires in 1 hour" or "your payment failed" forces a decision before you can verify it.

  • 03
    Pretexting

    A fabricated scenario — a fake IT ticket, a "returning" vendor, a courier delivery issue — used to justify an unusual request.

  • 04
    Baiting

    Free gift cards, prize wins, or a "found" USB drive left in a parking lot designed to exploit curiosity or greed.

  • 05
    Business Email Compromise (BEC)

    An attacker mimics or hijacks an executive's account to redirect a wire transfer or request confidential data.

  • 06
    Vishing & smishing

    The same tactics delivered by voice call or text message, often to bypass email security filters entirely.

Field Guide

Practical habits that stop phishing cold

You don't need to catch every fake detail — you need a few reflexes that work every time.

Hover before you click

Rest your cursor on any link to preview the real destination before committing to a click.

Verify through a second channel

Confirm unusual requests — especially payments — by phone or in person, using a number you already know, not one in the message.

Never reuse passwords

One stolen password shouldn't unlock every account. A password manager makes unique passwords painless.

Turn on multi-factor authentication

Even a captured password becomes far less useful to an attacker if a second factor is required.

Go straight to the source

Type the known address or use a saved bookmark instead of clicking a link in an email or text.

Report, don't just delete

Forwarding suspicious emails to your security team helps block the same attack from reaching your colleagues.

Closed Files

Real-world case studies

Click a file to open the record.

The $75M invoice swap

BEC +
Attackers compromised an executive's email and studied real vendor correspondence for weeks. They then sent finance staff a routine-looking invoice update with new bank details, timed to a real payment cycle. Because the request matched an ongoing, legitimate relationship, no one flagged it until the vendor called asking why they hadn't been paid.
Lesson: verify any change to payment details by phone, using a number from an existing contract — never from the email itself.

The cloned SSO portal

Credential theft +
Employees received a text about a "benefits enrollment deadline," linking to a single sign-on page that was a pixel-perfect copy of their real company portal, hosted on a similar-looking domain. Multiple employees entered credentials and their MFA code, which the fake site relayed to the real portal in real time to hijack the session.
Lesson: even MFA can be relayed by a fast attacker — always confirm the domain in the address bar before entering a code.

The "free" gift card

Baiting +
A message claiming to be from HR offered a holiday gift card, linking to a page asking for a work email and password "to verify eligibility." Dozens of employees, expecting a genuine reward program, entered their credentials on a page that had nothing to do with the company.
Lesson: unexpected rewards that require a login are one of the oldest tricks in the book — verify through HR directly first.
