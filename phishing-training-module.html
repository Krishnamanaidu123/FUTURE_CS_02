EXHIBIT A
The S.L.A.M. Inspection
Four checkpoints, run in order, before any click.

S — Sender

Open the real address behind the display name. Names are free to fake; domains are the tell.

L — Links

Hold your cursor over it, don't press. Does the preview URL match the words on screen?

A — Attachments

An unrequested .zip, .exe, .html, or macro-enabled .docm is a flag, not a formality.

M — Message

Manufactured urgency, a greeting with no name, or clumsy phrasing all point the same direction.

⚡ On the stand: “PayPal Security” <randombob123@gmail.com> — the name checks out, the domain doesn't. Case closed: forged.

EXHIBIT B
The Forged Storefront
A padlock 🔒 proves the connection is private. It proves nothing about who's on the other end.

Lookalike domains are the classic forgery: swap a letter, add a word, change the ending — g00gle.com instead of google.com.

Free SSL certificates are trivial to obtain, so scammers use them too. Encryption keeps the data private in transit — it says nothing about who's collecting it.

Forged
https://mybank-secure.xyz/login
Verified
https://www.mybank.com/login

Standing rule: never sign in through a link an email handed you. Type the address you already know into the bar yourself.

EXHIBIT C
The Five Levers
These attacks don't break the computer. They work on you.
👑 Authority ⏳ Urgency 🤝 Familiarity 💎 Scarcity 😨 Fear

Wire-fraud impersonation: a message that looks like it's from leadership, demanding a same-day payment before anyone can double-check it.

Push-notification fatigue: an attacker who already has your password floods your phone with login approvals until one gets tapped just to make the noise stop.

EXHIBIT D
Two Closed Cases
Real patterns, reduced to what they teach.

🎁 The Gift-Card Ask

A message arrives that looks like it's from a manager, requesting $1,000 in gift cards for "client gifts," today, quietly.

What happened: the sender name was spoofed. The employee bought the cards and the funds were unrecoverable.
Takeaway: money requests get confirmed by phone, on a number you already had — never one from the message itself.

📄 The Shared-Document Trap

An invite arrives to "edit a shared document" from what looks like a known contact.

What happened: accepting it granted a third-party app permission to the victim's account and contact list — no password was ever typed.
Takeaway: permission prompts deserve the same scrutiny as a login page. Unrecognized app, no grant.
EXHIBIT E
The Field Kit
Four habits that hold up against most of what's out there.
01App-based multi-factor authentication. An authenticator app or hardware key resists interception in a way a text message doesn't.
02A password manager as a lie detector. It won't autofill on a domain it doesn't recognize — silence is the warning.
03Verification out-of-band. Any request for money or credentials gets confirmed through a channel the message didn't provide.
04Report, don't just delete. One reported message can get a whole organization's filters updated before the next copy lands.
